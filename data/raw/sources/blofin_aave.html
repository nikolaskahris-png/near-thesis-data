    # AAVE

    AAVE Buybacks and Aavenomics: Where Purchased Tokens Go

    BloFin Academy10/29/2026

    You read that a crypto protocol is spending part of its fee income to buy its own token, and the post calls it a burn that shrinks supply for good. Before you trade on that story, you need to know who placed the orders, which wallet received the tokens, and whether any of them were actually destroyed.

    The term comes from stock buybacks, where a company repurchases its own shares on the open market and then either retires them or holds them as treasury stock (source: Investopedia, Stock Buybacks). Token buybacks face the same choice, and that choice decides whether supply really falls or the tokens simply change owners.

    The AAVE token has a program of this kind, the Aavenomics buyback, and it takes the second option: the Aave DAO keeps what it buys and spends it again later.

    What are Aavenomics buybacks?

    Aavenomics buybacks are open-market purchases of AAVE paid for with protocol revenue. Approved in August 2024, they were executed by the Aave Finance Committee from April 2025 (source: Aave Docs, AAVE Token). The committee's buying stopped on April 19, 2026, after the rsETH incident (source: Aave Governance, Pause AAVE Buybacks).

    The Aave DAO is the decentralized autonomous organization of AAVE holders that votes on changes to the Aave Protocol and controls its treasury. Aavenomics is the name for the token economics it runs on, and the framework began with the migration from LEND to AAVE, where every 100 LEND converted into 1 AAVE, which turned 1.3 billion LEND into 16 million AAVE. Of those 16 million tokens, 13 million were set aside for LEND holders to redeem and 3 million went to the Aave Ecosystem Reserve to pay for protocol incentives, with AAVE holders deciding how that reserve is split between safety and ecosystem rewards (source: Aave GitHub, Aavenomics README).

    The 2024 update changed where those rewards come from. The DAO earns revenue mainly from borrow fees and liquidation fees. The buyback turns part of that revenue into AAVE that funds Safety Module emissions, service-provider pay and future business initiatives (source: Aave Governance, Funding Insights).

    The Safety Module is the staking pool where stkAAVE holders earn AAVE rewards (source: Aave Docs, AAVE Token). It was built as a backstop against shortfalls, a role Umbrella is meant to take over. Slashing on stkAAVE was already 0% by February 2026 (source: Aave Governance, Funding Insights). Governance cut the module's AAVE emissions in March 2026, partly so that future rewards could be funded through buybacks (source: Aave Governance, AIP 459). Buybacks became part of AAVE tokenomics through the 2024 update, next to the fixed supply and the staking rewards set at the migration.

    How the Aave Finance Committee ran buybacks

    The implementation proposal set up the Aave Finance Committee (AFC) as a four-member multisig that needed three of the four signatures for any transaction. Under that proposal, the committee bought AAVE on secondary markets, directly or through market-making partners, before sending the purchased tokens to the Ecosystem Reserve (source: Aave Governance, Aavenomics Part one).

    A multisig is a wallet controlled by several keys, where a set number of key holders must approve a transaction before it executes. With a 3-of-4 threshold, one member could not move funds alone, and the committee could still act when one member was unavailable. At approval, the four members were existing Aave DAO service providers (source: Aave Governance, Aavenomics Part one). The buying therefore sat with the DAO, and the difference between Aave Labs, the protocol, and the token matters whenever a headline credits the wrong party.

    The AFC spent through token allowances that TokenLogic granted during its monthly treasury-management proposals, and TokenLogic also chose, month to month, which treasury tokens financed the purchases. The program opened at $1 million of AAVE a week for its first six months. After that period, TokenLogic was to size a quarterly buyback budget against the protocol's overall budget, which would then become the allowance the committee could execute (source: Aave Governance, Aavenomics Part one).

    Inside a budget, the AFC set its own weekly pace. An October 2025 proposal set a weekly execution range of $250,000 to $1,750,000 of AAVE purchases and let the committee vary volume within a 75% range, based on market conditions and liquidity, token price volatility, timing and available protocol revenue (source: Aave Governance, Buybacks update). That range works out to the original $1 million weekly base moved up or down by 75%: $1,000,000 × 0.25 = $250,000 and $1,000,000 × 1.75 = $1,750,000.

    Here is what that meant for one week at an AAVE price of $200. At the base pace, $1,000,000 ÷ $200 bought 5,000 AAVE. In a week of thin order books and sharp price swings, the committee could drop to the floor and buy $250,000 ÷ $200 = 1,250 AAVE, then buy up to 8,750 AAVE in a calmer week at the ceiling. Those rules applied until the committee's buying was paused on April 19, 2026.

    Where purchased AAVE goes in the Ecosystem Reserve

    Purchased AAVE was sent to the Aave DAO Ecosystem Reserve, the DAO's AAVE treasury that funds grants, staking rewards and payments to service providers. Tokens leave the reserve only through governance-approved programs. A buyback therefore moves AAVE from sellers' wallets into a DAO-held balance that can be paid out again later (source: Aave Docs, AAVE Token).

    The reserve took in a second, one-time inflow in 2026. On May 8, 2026, Aave governance executed proposal 480, which closed the LEND to AAVE migration contract after more than half a decade and redirected the roughly 302,000 AAVE still inside it to the Ecosystem Reserve. Moving those tokens to the DAO treasury made them available for future spending that governance directs. A later proposal is expected to allocate them to growth and ecosystem incentives (source: Aave Governance, Proposal 480).

    Outflows follow the same voting process. Staking rewards for stkAAVE holders are one of them, and liquidity incentives are another. When the implementation proposal was written, the DAO was spending about $27 million a year of reserve AAVE, at then-current prices, on secondary-market liquidity incentives. The proposal sought to move part of that budget to the Aave Liquidity Committee through a reserve allowance that lets it distribute rewards more efficiently (source: Aave Governance, Aavenomics Part one).

    The reserve balance is the net of those flows, which you can work through with round numbers. Suppose a week of buying delivers 5,000 AAVE to the reserve, and the same week governance-approved programs pay out 1,800 AAVE in staking rewards and 700 AAVE in a grant. The reserve ends the week 5,000 − 1,800 − 700 = 2,500 AAVE larger, while 2,500 AAVE are now in the hands of stakers and the grant recipient, who can hold them or sell them.

    Buyback vs burn and AAVE total supply

    A token burn sends tokens to an address nobody can spend from, or reduces the supply recorded in the token contract, so the reduction is permanent. An Aavenomics buyback keeps every purchased token for governance-approved programs and burns none of them, which keeps AAVE's total supply where it was (source: Aave Docs, AAVE Token).

    Follow 1,000 AAVE through a buyback to see which numbers move. The sellers' balances fall by 1,000 AAVE while the Ecosystem Reserve rises by 1,000 AAVE. The maximum total supply on the token contract stays at 16,000,000 AAVE throughout (source: Etherscan, AAVE token). The only thing that changed is who holds those 1,000 tokens.

    Circulating supply is where the two measures differ. When a data provider counts treasury balances as non-circulating, a buyback lowers the circulating figure on its dashboard while total supply holds still, and a later payout from the reserve raises the circulating figure again. Two dashboards that treat the reserve differently can therefore report different circulating supply for AAVE on the same day, and both can be accurate.

    The common mistake is reading a buyback announcement as a supply cut. Suppose a post says the DAO bought 10,000 AAVE this month and calls it 10,000 tokens burned. Check the token contract first: if total supply still reads 16,000,000, nothing was burned, and those 10,000 AAVE sit in the reserve, where a later grant or reward program can return them to circulation. Treating them as gone overstates scarcity, which matters if you value the token on its supply.

    A real burn of reserve AAVE would need its own governance approval and a transaction that sends the tokens to an unspendable address or reduces total supply, and you would see the total fall on the token contract.

    Program timeline and budget updates

    The program moved through dated votes. Aavenomics buybacks launched on April 9, 2025 (source: Aave Governance, Funding Insights). An October 22, 2025 proposal set $50 million a year with no end date (source: Aave Governance, Buybacks update). A March 2026 proposal sought about $30 million (source: Aave Governance, Budget Adjustment).

    The first 10 months give a sense of scale. Over that period the DAO allocated $42 million to buying about 205,000 AAVE, more than 1.28% of total supply (source: Aave Governance, Funding Insights). Dividing one figure by the other gives the average cost: $42,000,000 ÷ 205,000 ≈ $204.88 per AAVE, so roughly $205 a token across those months.

    The case for a smaller budget rested on revenue. Borrow-fee income, the DAO's most reliable revenue stream, had fallen about 25% from its peak as interest rates compressed, while service-provider costs were rising, and the $50 million budget had been set when cash flow and asset prices were strong. The update therefore proposed $30 million a year to direct money toward GHO liquidity and other growth programs. It also proposed paying for some purchases with ETH alongside stablecoins (source: Aave Governance, Funding Insights).

    The March 4, 2026 proposal turned that recommendation into a formal request. It estimated that a $30 million budget would still buy about 292 AAVE a day, against about 487 a day at $50 million. It also named ETH and ETH-correlated assets as the main source of funds (source: Aave Governance, Budget Adjustment). To turn an annual budget into a weekly pace, divide by 52. A $50 million year averaged about $962,000 a week and a $30 million year about $577,000, so both fell inside the weekly execution range. A cut would have shown up mostly as slower average buying, with the tokens still going to the Ecosystem Reserve.

    The committee's buying then stopped. On April 18, 2026, an exploit on the LayerZero bridge route for Kelp's rsETH let unbacked rsETH into Aave V3 markets on several chains. Buybacks were paused from April 19 so the treasury kept the flexibility to fund a coordinated response. None had been executed since that date when the pause was formalized on April 22, 2026. The pause was to last until the rsETH situation became clearer, with any restart to be announced in the regular funding update (source: Aave Governance, Pause AAVE Buybacks). Buybacks were still paused in October 2026. The last purchase was made on April 19, 2026 (source: TokenLogic, Aave Financials buyback dashboard). Treat every buyback figure as a dated number tied to a specific vote.

    Aave buybacks and AAVE price

    Until the pause, the buyback added a steady, rule-based buyer to the AAVE market, funded by protocol revenue and meant in part to create consistent buying pressure for the token (source: Aave Governance, Buybacks update). Price still answered to every other buyer and seller, so a growing reserve described the DAO's treasury and set no price for AAVE.

    Stock markets show the same limit. A stock buyback reduces the shares available on the open market and can lift the price. Yet a stock can still fall while a buyback is underway, and some investors read a buyback as a sign that the company has run short of better uses for its cash (source: Investopedia, Stock Buybacks). The same reading applies to a DAO treasury, where every dollar spent on AAVE is a dollar that does not go to incentives or development.

    The other AAVE price drivers include the direction of bitcoin and ether, staking flows, governance news, and changes in protocol revenue, and in a given week any of them can matter more than a budget change. Buyback size, including whether buying is paused at all, is one input you can measure and date, which is why it is worth tracking next to the others.

    How to check an Aave buyback claim

    To check a buyback claim, follow six steps: establish the dated governance record and current budget, compare total supply and reserve transfers, then examine outflows and what the evidence means for you. These checks separate an authorized purchase from a supply reduction, since a transfer into the Ecosystem Reserve does not itself burn AAVE.

    1. Find the dated source. Open the forum post or proposal behind the figure and note its date, its author, and whether it passed, since a forum post becomes binding only after an Aave governance vote on chain.
    2. Compare the figure with the latest budget. A weekly or annual number from an earlier proposal may have been replaced, so check the most recent funding update before you treat it as current, and vote with your AAVE if you hold tokens and want a say in the next one.
    3. Read total supply on the AAVE token contract. If it still shows the same maximum supply, the purchase was a transfer and nothing was burned.
    4. Look for the transfer into the Ecosystem Reserve. The committee's buying stopped on April 19, 2026, so a claim of AFC purchases after that date needs its own governance record. A purchase that never reaches the reserve is a claim you cannot confirm yet.
    5. Check outflows over the same period. Staking rewards and grants paid from the reserve put tokens back in circulation, which offsets part of the buying.
    6. Decide what it means for your position. A buyback, while it runs, is one steady buyer among many, so weigh it next to market direction and protocol revenue before you size a trade.

    Run those six steps whenever a buyback headline makes a supply claim, and keep the date of each figure you rely on. Steps 1 and 2 establish the authorization and budget; steps 3 to 5 separate supply changes from reserve movements. Step 6 is your interpretation of that evidence, not another transaction that proves a buyback occurred.

    Frequently asked questions

    Why did Aave choose buy and distribute over buy and burn?

    The DAO pays out AAVE every year to stakers and service providers, and the long-term Aavenomics goal was to source all of that distribution through tokens bought on secondary markets. TokenLogic was to size the buybacks against the protocol's overall budget with the aim of eventually matching, and even surpassing, all protocol AAVE spending (source: Aave Governance, Aavenomics Part one). Burning purchased tokens would push those payouts onto the reserve's existing balance, so recycling them through the reserve lets revenue pay for rewards while the total number of tokens stays fixed.

    Why did the Aave DAO plan to pay for buybacks with ETH?

    ETH and AAVE tend to move together, so swapping ETH for AAVE shifts the treasury's mix between two correlated assets, while buying with stablecoins adds new directional exposure to AAVE. Most AAVE liquidity on decentralized exchanges is paired with ETH, so paying in ETH skips the extra swap from stablecoins into ETH and cuts slippage, gas costs, and MEV (maximal extractable value) exposure. Part of the DAO's income already arrives in ETH: liquidation-fee revenue in February 2026 included 2,253.4 ETH, worth about $4.5 million at $2,000 per ETH (source: Aave Governance, Funding Insights).

    What else does the AFC manage besides buybacks?

    The committee was formed to manage the Aave collector contracts, where the DAO's treasury assets sit, and to set Umbrella liquidity targets (source: Aave Governance, Aavenomics Part one). Umbrella is the newer system replacing the Safety Module's bad-debt coverage. A later mandate let the AFC use AAVE, wBTC, and wETH reserves as collateral to borrow for growth initiatives. Those positions had to keep a health factor, Aave's measure of how far a borrowing position is from liquidation, above 2 (source: Aave Governance, Buybacks update). As of February 2026, the AFC also governed the Aave Helper Asset Budget (Ahab), the DAO's partnership and growth budget (source: Aave Governance, Funding Insights).

    Can stkAAVE stakers be slashed?

    As of February 2026, slashing for stkAAVE was set at 0%, which meant staked AAVE would not be used to cover a shortfall (source: Aave Governance, Funding Insights). Since AIP 459 executed on March 23, 2026, the stkAAVE cooldown has been 2 days, down from 7, a change meant to make stkAAVE more liquid. The same vote moved the stkAAVE/wstETH BPTv2 pool, which holds Balancer AAVE and wstETH liquidity tokens, from 10% slashing and a 20-day cooldown to 0% slashing, no cooldown and no emissions (source: Aave Governance, AIP 459).

    How large is the buyback compared with AAVE's market value?

    A common measure is the buyback ratio, which divides a year of buyback spending by market capitalization at the start of the period (source: Investopedia, Stock Buybacks). On October 1, 2026, AAVE traded at $164.16 with an on-chain market cap of about $2.63 billion (source: Etherscan, AAVE token). The October 2025 budget of $50 million would equal 50 ÷ 2,630 ≈ 0.019, or about 1.9 cents of buying per dollar of market value each year. The $30 million proposed in March 2026 would come to about 1.1 cents. Both are full-year hypotheticals, since the April 19, 2026 pause halted AFC purchases (source: Aave Governance, Pause AAVE Buybacks).

    Was the Aave DAO buying more AAVE than it paid out?

    As of February 2026, the DAO was acquiring more AAVE than it distributed, because the buyback budget at that time was worth more than the AAVE emissions paid to Safety Module stakers and service providers (source: Aave Governance, Funding Insights). Emissions then fell when AIP 459 ended rewards for the stkAAVE/wstETH BPTv2 pool on March 23, 2026, saving 14,600 AAVE a year (source: Aave Governance, AIP 459). That surplus depended on the committee's buying, which was paused on April 19, 2026. Comparing the reserve's AAVE balance on a block explorer across two dates shows the net direction for any period.
